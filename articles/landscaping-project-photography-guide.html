Landscaping project photos: a trustworthy portfolio without a studio

A simple repeatable shot list, permission workflow and caption method for contractors who want to show real work instead of stock-photo promises.

SERVICE STACK EDITORIAL DESK · NOV 13, 2026 · DOCUMENTATION-BASED GUIDE

Editorial transparency: This guide is based on linked official documentation and an original editorial decision framework, not hands-on testing or measured customer results. No paid referral links are included. Product features, prices and policies may change. All images are synthetic editorial illustrations, never real client work. Our editorial standards.

The short answer: A small set of authentic, permissioned, well-captioned photos beats a large gallery of generic garden images. Photograph the problem, the work and the result from comparable viewpoints.

In this guide:

  1. Decide what each photo must prove
  2. Use a consistent four-frame field routine
  3. Get permission before publication
  4. Write captions that add facts
  5. Make the gallery useful on mobile

Decide what each photo must prove

A project photo should help a buyer answer a specific question: can this contractor handle a slope, install a patio edge, or improve drainage without destroying the rest of the garden? Pretty greenery alone rarely answers that. Start each project with a one-sentence job description and a checklist of the details worth showing.

For a patio, photograph the original condition, ground preparation if safe, edge and drainage details, and the completed surface. For a planting job, show the site context, plant spacing and a clearly dated finished view. A later-season photo can be useful, but do not imply it was taken immediately after installation.

Use a consistent four-frame field routine

Frame one: a wide view from a safe, repeatable position. Frame two: the principal problem or work area. Frame three: a construction detail that demonstrates care without exposing proprietary or private information. Frame four: the finished result from approximately the first viewpoint.

Clean the lens, use daylight where possible and keep the camera level. Avoid heavy HDR, exaggerated saturation or AI removal of defects. A modest phone camera with an honest composition can communicate more than a dramatic render that misrepresents the completed job.

Worked example: four shots of one fictional patio project

Illustrative shot list only: the following captions and alt-text samples describe a hypothetical project, not photographs we took, client results or evidence that any contractor performed the work. Replace every detail with verified facts before using the template in a real portfolio.

  1. Before — site context. Sample caption: “Before work: a small paved courtyard with a worn central path, viewed from the garden gate.” Sample alt: “Wide view of a hypothetical courtyard with an uneven paved path and a fence.” Photograph from a safe, repeatable position and record the date.
  2. Problem — detail. Sample caption: “Closer view of the uneven paving that prompted the request; cause not established by this photo.” Sample alt: “Close-up of uneven paving joints in the example courtyard.” Avoid diagnosing drainage or structural faults from appearance alone.
  3. During — documented work. Sample caption: “Example construction-stage view of the prepared surface before new paving; method and materials require crew confirmation.” Sample alt: “Hypothetical patio base preparation with edging visible.” Only share safe, authorized site images; never invent concealed construction details.
  4. After — comparable viewpoint. Sample caption: “Example completed patio photographed from the same gate position; actual completion date and materials must be verified.” Sample alt: “Hypothetical finished paved courtyard with a planted border and timber fence.” Match camera position and lighting where possible, and do not imply that an AI-generated image is a real finished job.

Publishing gate: pair actual before-and-after files from the same authorized project, obtain written channel-specific permission, confirm dates/materials with the crew and remove identifying property details. If a frame is missing, label the sequence incomplete rather than fabricating one.

Get permission before publication

Obtain the property owner's permission for identifiable private areas and any people in the frame. Record the scope of consent: website, social media, paid advertising or only a private quote. Where workers are identifiable, confirm the appropriate consent or other legal basis before publishing.

Do not include house numbers, vehicle registration plates, children or documents in a public gallery. Keep a simple permission log with project ID, date, approved channels and the person's preferred contact method. If permission is withdrawn, have a process to remove the material from all channels you control.

Write captions that add facts

A useful caption might say: “Small courtyard patio replacement; permeable edge treatment and access constraints described by the crew; photographed after completion.” Include town or materials only when verified and permissioned. Avoid inventing square footage, price or duration.

Alt text should describe the visible image for someone who cannot see it: “Completed stone patio with a narrow planted border and timber fence.” Do not stuff five town names into alt text. Google provides separate image guidance, but accessibility and truthful description come first.

Make the gallery useful on mobile

Choose a representative cover photo and arrange supporting photos in a sequence. Compress files without destroying construction detail. Use meaningful filenames, correct width and height attributes and lazy loading for images below the first screen.

Open the gallery at 390px and 1280px. Check whether captions remain attached to their images, whether a tap opens the correct photo and whether the user can return to the quote form. A gallery that makes the customer hunt for contact information is an expensive detour.

A practical pre-launch decision checklist

Use these questions with your actual business workflow, not with a demo or a hypothetical sales forecast. A “yes” should mean you have verified it with your own site, customer journey or authorized business records.

  • Are all project photos real and permissioned?
  • Are before/after images from the same job and comparable viewpoints?
  • Do captions avoid invented details?
  • Does the mobile gallery keep contact actions accessible?

If any answer is unknown, assign a real person to test it before spending money or promising customers a result. For a related starting point, see our landscaping homepage guide and quote-request form guide.
